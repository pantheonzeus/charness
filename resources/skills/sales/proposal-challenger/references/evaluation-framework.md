# Evaluation Framework & Failure Patterns

Read this when you want the fuller method behind the critique — how to extract the right things from an RFP, how to judge whether a proposal truly answers it, and the recurring ways proposals lose. SKILL.md has the workflow; this is the depth.

## Part 1 — Reading the RFP like the buyer

The proposal team wrote their document looking outward (here's what we offer). Your job is to read looking inward from the client's chair (here's what we'll buy, and how we'll choose). Reconstruct the client's decision before you judge the proposal.

### The scoring rubric is the spine

If the RFP states evaluation criteria, those criteria *are* the test. Everything else is secondary. Three things to nail:

- **Order and weight.** "In roughly this order of importance" is a gift — it tells you exactly where to concentrate. A brilliant answer to criterion #5 cannot compensate for a missing answer to criterion #1. Weight your findings the same way the client weights their decision.
- **What each criterion actually means.** "Credibility and chemistry with the proposed team" is not the same as "relevant experience." The first is about people and fit (often decided in a meeting or interview), the second about track record (decided on paper). Read each criterion for what evidence would satisfy it.
- **Soft criteria are real criteria.** Chemistry, trust, cultural fit, "ability to scale the engagement up or down" — these look fuzzy but they decide deals. A proposal that ignores them because they're hard to evidence is leaving points on the table.

### Required outcomes are checkboxes

When the RFP lists what the work must produce or the proposal must contain, treat each item as a checkbox and tick it against the proposal. Missing checkboxes are the cheapest possible finding to surface and the easiest for the team to fix — never skip this pass. Common required-content items proposals forget: pricing model and payment terms, team composition with named people, references with concrete outcomes, timeline with milestones and decision points, assumptions/risks, IP and data-handling terms.

### Embedded questions

RFPs hide direct questions in prose. "How would you involve our leadership, business units, experts and possibly customers?" is four questions in one sentence. Pull every question out and check each is answered. An unanswered embedded question reads, to the evaluator, as either an oversight or an evasion — both lose points.

### The client's language and self-image

How does the client describe their own problem, their organisation, and what success looks like? The exact nouns and verbs matter. A proposal that mirrors the client's language ("predictive, proactive, consultative service", "grey-economy enforcement", "expert work") signals understanding; one that overwrites it with vendor frameworks ("our AI Transformation methodology, phase 1: Opportunity Discovery") signals a recycled template. Flag vendor-jargon-over-client-language wherever the proposal does this.

### Invitations and tests

Watch for:
- **Explicit invitations to challenge** ("feel free to propose different questions"). Taking these up is a differentiator; ignoring them is a missed signal.
- **Working sessions / interviews / shortlists** mentioned in the process ("the two best bidders are invited to a working session with our leadership team"). The proposal can and should pre-empt these — e.g. by proposing the format of that session, which demonstrates the working style instead of just describing it.
- **Optionality the client signals** ("we reserve the right to award partially", "scale up or down as we learn"). This is usually a request for modular pricing and a flexible engagement shape. A monolithic take-it-or-leave-it proposal misreads it.

## Part 2 — Judging the proposal against it

For each thing extracted from the RFP, the proposal lands in one of four states:

1. **Answered well** — addressed clearly, specifically, in the client's terms, in a way that scores. Note briefly; move on.
2. **Answered weakly** — touched but generic, buried, hedged, or in vendor language. These are often the highest-leverage fixes because the raw material is there.
3. **Not answered** — the RFP asks, the proposal is silent. Gap.
4. **Answered, but the wrong question** — the proposal confidently addresses something adjacent to, but not, what was asked. The most dangerous state, because it *feels* complete to the team that wrote it.

Severity is a function of (a) how heavily the RFP weights the thing and (b) how badly the proposal handles it. Top-criterion + not-answered = critical. Low-priority + answered-weakly = tactical. Sort accordingly.

## Part 3 — Recurring failure patterns

These show up across industries. Use them as a checklist of things to actively look for.

- **Template recycling.** The proposal reads like it was built for a previous client with names swapped. Tell: vendor's own framework names dominate; the client's specific vocabulary is absent; the structure doesn't follow the RFP's structure.
- **Answering the wrong question / wrong headline.** The lead message fights the client's stated need (selling speed when they asked for focus; selling technology when they asked about people and change). Re-read the RFP's situation/diagnosis section and check the proposal's headline against it.
- **Org-chart-as-strategy.** The proposal leads with how work will be structured (governance, operating model, who sits where) when the RFP's actual priority is an outcome (better service, changed expert work, customer value). Structure belongs later; it's not the headline.
- **Capability list instead of value chain.** The proposal lists what it can do, but never connects those capabilities to the client's value-creation logic and commercial outcomes. RFPs increasingly ask for the *chain* (people → tools → service quality → customer value → profitable growth), and a proposal that doesn't draw it leaves the central question open.
- **No proof.** Claims of impact with no references, no named outcomes, no metrics. If a criterion is "evidence you can deliver real impact," assertions don't satisfy it.
- **Process described, not demonstrated.** "We are collaborative and practical" stated, never shown. Where the RFP offers a working session or interview, the proposal can *demonstrate* the working style; describing it is weaker.
- **Governance/compliance as a bolt-on.** Treated as an administrative afterthought when, for some clients (public sector, regulated, trust-critical), it's brand-critical and a genuine differentiator if handled as part of the value proposition.
- **Missing the human/change dimension.** Especially in transformation work, RFPs ask how roles, management, skills, and ways of working change. Tech-only proposals miss this even when the RFP asks directly.
- **No modular commercial shape.** When the client signals they may award partially or want to scale, a single fixed price with no decision points/phasing misreads the request and looks inflexible.
- **Generic everything.** Could be sent to any client in the sector. The fix is always specificity: this client, these business units, this named constraint, this person.

## Part 4 — The competitor lens

Even without knowing the exact bidders, you can usually reason about the field from the RFP and the client:

- **Who plausibly bids** for this kind of work (large incumbents, boutiques, specialists, the client's existing vendors)?
- **What's each archetype's natural advantage** (brand and breadth; price and speed; deep domain; existing relationship)?
- **Where does this proposal beat them, and where is it exposed?** Concede the fights you can't win (don't out-compliance a Big-4 on compliance) and press the ones you can (e.g. ability to actually build, not just advise; speed; cultural fit).
- **How would the strongest competitor attack this exact proposal?** Then make sure the proposal pre-empts that attack.

Keep this grounded. If you genuinely don't know the market, say "I don't know who else is bidding, but here's the archetype analysis" rather than inventing specific company names and claims.

## Part 5 — Tone and the relationship

The team wants to win and asked to be challenged, so directness is a service, not an attack. But directness without a path forward is just demoralising. Every critical finding should carry the seed of the fix. The implicit contract: "I'm going to be hard on this because you asked me to help you win, and here's exactly what to do about each thing." Maintain that throughout — hard on the proposal, on the side of the team.
